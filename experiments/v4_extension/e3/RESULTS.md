# E3 — simulated response (offline mitigation replay): results

**This is an offline, simulated mitigation measurement.** The 1,692,332 recorded Run B test
events were replayed in time order, and blocks were applied to the replay. No network device was
controlled, no traffic was dropped, and nothing was retrained. Run 2026-11-16 UTC. Commands,
checks and the design decisions (fixed before the first run) are in `RUN_LOG.md`. All numbers come
from `results/` (tables generated by `report_e3.py` → `results/tables.md`).

**Setup.**
- **Events:** the same 1,692,332 Run B test events as E1/E2: 1,569,525 attack events, of which
  1,250,540 are DoS and 318,985 are not; and 122,807 benign events. There are 175,489 incidents,
  64,243 of them with an attack event.
- **Replay order:** (ts, event_id) within each source capture.
- **Triggers:**
  - A/C: `pred_fragmented`, one alert at every predicted-attack event.
  - B: `pred_integrated`, one alert at the last event of each predicted-attack incident, as in E2.
  - event_only and event_plus_thin: the seed-42 ablation predictions.
  - Oracle: the true label. **Not achievable**; it is an upper bound.
  - No response: the lower bound.
- **Policies:**
  - P1 blocks the flow (source, src_ip, dst_ip, proto).
  - P2 quarantines the host (source, src_ip).
- **Blocks:**
  - Each block lasts 60 s, 300 s, 900 s or until the end of the capture, as `[t, t + D)`.
  - A new alert on the same key restarts the block from its own time.
  - The event that raises the alert is never counted as prevented.
- **Checks** (`results/checks.json`):
  - The join covers all 1,692,332 events, with unique ids and matching incident, label and ts.
  - Every Run B incident is one flow and one host.
  - **C = A:** C triggers on the same event positions as A, and its blocked events equal A's for
    all 8 policy × duration settings. The two are therefore reported as one row, A/C.
- **Input hashes:** 164 files (the 53 Run B / Run T inputs, E1/E2 outputs and `predictions/`)
  are identical before and after (`results/inputs_sha256_{before,after}.txt`).

Column meanings:
- **Attack prevented:** blocked attack events / all attack test events.
- **Wrongly blocked:** blocked benign events / all benign test events.
- **Benign hosts:** distinct (source, src_ip) with ≥ 1 benign event blocked.
- **Contained ≥ 90% / None blocked:** among the 58,897 attack incidents with ≥ 2 attack events,
  those with ≥ 90% / 0% of the attack events after their first one blocked. The other 5,346 attack
  incidents have a single attack event.
- **Never blocked:** attack incidents with no block on their key at or after their first attack event.
- **Time to block:** the seconds from an incident's first attack event to the first block covering
  its key. It is 0 if a block was already active. It is computed over incidents that get a block.

Source files:
- `results/response_all.csv`: every trigger × policy × duration.
- `results/response_breakdown.csv`: per class and per dataset.
- `results/response_per_capture.csv` and `per_capture_wins.json`.
- `results/bootstrap.json`.
- `results/blocked_300s.parquet`: the per-event blocked flags at 300 s for every trigger and policy.

## Why the numbers look like this (`results/diagnostics.json`)

These are explanatory checks, written after the main run; no result changed.

- **B can rarely act on a flow in time.**
  - 170,445 of the 171,489 flows contain only one incident. They hold 90.6% of the attack events.
  - B alerts when an incident ends, and by then its flow usually has no traffic left.
  - So under P1, B prevents 0.66% (300 s). Its median time to block is 15.0 s, the same
    ~15 s incident length seen in E2.
- **The first event of every flow cannot be prevented.**
  - The trigger event is never counted, so even the oracle stops only 95.96% under P1.
  - IoT-23 reconnaissance contacts many destinations once each, which caps P1 at 79.9% for recon
    and 64.1% for IoT-23 overall, for every event-level trigger and for the oracle.
- **Host quarantine blocks most harmless events, even for the oracle.**
  - 94.7% of the benign test events come from the 23 hosts that also send attack traffic. Most are
    in CTU-IoT-Malware-Capture-1-1 (116,802 of 122,807 benign events).
  - So P2 wrongly blocks 92.6% of benign events under the oracle as well. Any correct detection
    leads to quarantining infected devices that also send normal traffic. The cost falls on
    14–21 hosts, depending on trigger and duration.
- **B misses DoS because a few DoS incidents are huge.**
  - The 13 DoS incidents hold all 1,250,540 DoS events. The largest has 271,637 events.
  - 97.7% of DoS events occur before B's first alert on their host. B therefore prevents 2.3% of DoS
    under P2.
  - Its 83.8% non-DoS figure under P2 mainly comes from blocks raised by earlier incidents of the
    same host.
  - The median B time to block of 0 s under P2 has the same cause: the host is usually already
    blocked by an earlier B alert.

## Plain-English summary

- **Acting on event alerts stops most later attack traffic; acting on finished incidents mostly
  comes too late.**
  - Per flow at 300 s, A/C prevents 95.95% of attack events (80.10% without DoS) and wrongly blocks
    0.76% of benign events.
  - B prevents 0.66% (3.26% without DoS) and wrongly blocks 0.23%.
  - The oracle bound is 95.96% (80.10% without DoS).
  - A/C − B: +95.3 pp prevented [+87.0, +97.4]; wrongly blocked +0.53 pp [+0.34, +0.72].
    B wrongly blocks less because it blocks almost nothing.
- **The two ablation triggers behave like A/C.** event_only and event_plus_thin are within
  0.05 pp of A/C on prevention. They wrongly block 0.80% and 0.77% under P1.
- **Host quarantine raises prevention to almost 100% but blocks over 92% of harmless events,
  whatever the trigger, the oracle included.**
  - At 300 s, A/C − B under P2: +81.1 pp prevented [+53.3, +90.0]; wrongly blocked −0.41 pp
    [−0.73, −0.14].
  - B's longer delay lets more DoS through, but B quarantines slightly more benign traffic.
- **Block duration matters little for event-level triggers.** For B it matters more: P1 rises
  from 0.15% (60 s) to 8.31% (end); P2 from 12.96% to 22.05%.
- **Per capture (300 s), A/C prevents more than B in 8 of the 9 captures with attacks under both
  policies.**
  - P1: one tie, in Capture-21-1, which has a single attack event.
  - P2: B wins in Capture-21-1.
  - Wrongly blocked is mixed. P1: A/C fewer in 3 of 13 captures, more in 3, tie in 7. P2: fewer in
    5, more in 6, tie in 2.
- **Time to block:** median 0.0 s for every event-level trigger (p90 0.0 s). For B under P1 it is
  15.0 s (p90 15.0 s), and 4,781 of the 64,243 attack incidents never get a block.

## Limitations

- **Offline replay.** Blocking in a real network would change attacker and device behaviour (retries,
  new ports, new hosts, lost legitimate service). Later events might not happen as recorded, and
  alerts from blocked traffic are assumed to keep coming (RUN_LOG D5).
- **Only test events are replayed.** Run B interleaves train and test within each capture, so traffic
  between test events (the training events) is not in the replay. Blocks would also have acted
  on that traffic.
- **Lab captures; Bot-IoT DoS dominates.** All data are IoT-23 and Bot-IoT lab captures. DoS floods
  in two Bot-IoT captures are 79.7% of attack events, so overall prevention figures are driven by
  DoS. The excluding-DoS and per-capture tables should be read alongside them. Benign traffic is
  concentrated in one capture (Capture-1-1).
- **No real control and no cost model.** No firewall, switch or device was controlled, and no risk,
  service disruption or operational cost was measured. "Wrongly blocked" counts events, not their
  importance. The bootstrap holds block outcomes fixed.
- **Other limits.**
  - Predictions come from one seed for the ablation triggers and from the saved Run B models for
    A/C and B.
  - Blocking keys use the recorded addresses, and a blocked host cannot change address.
  - Each capture is replayed alone, so there is no cross-sensor response.
